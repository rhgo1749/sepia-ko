# Korean calibration for the style pass

Load this file at the style-pass step whenever the target text is Korean, in any register. It changes nothing else about the route. The English ban lists in `style-pass.md` §3 do not transfer word for word; what transfers is the *shape* of the checks — the syntax templates of §2, the restore list of §4, the rhythm check of §5, the whitelist of §7 — and this file says what each shape looks like in Korean.

Evidence: the KatFishNet study — Park et al., 2025, "Detecting LLM-Generated Korean Text through Linguistic Feature Analysis", ACL 2025 (arXiv:2503.00032) — the first benchmark for LLM-generated Korean text (KatFish: 470 human + 1,624 LLM answers across argumentative essays, poetry and paper abstracts, generated by GPT-4o, Solar, Qwen2 72B and Llama3.1 70B). Its quantitative findings are measured; everything in §4 below is a Sepia inference marked as such. A second measured corpus at the benchmark level is KoLing (arXiv:2610.22940), a bias-controlled adversarial benchmark of 50.9k LLM-generated Korean texts; its purpose is robust detection, not style rewriting, so it contributes no surgical rule and no numbers here.

## 0 Scope and venue

Apply on Korean-language target text. The venue sets the register (formal/informal, honorific, formal vs. speech-like); the author's own samples decide whether the register rows apply when no venue samples exist; a venue by itself never licenses a stylistic change the text does not need. Every fix below applies to running prose only: code spans, commands and flags, Markdown syntax, URLs, file names, version strings and quoted material are never touched (the `SKILL.md` quoted-material guardrail).

Korean commas: 쉼표(,). Punctuation density is not a signal in any language (whitelist §7) — the shape-level check applies, the raw count does not.

## 1 Measured (KatFishNet / ACL 2025)

### 1a Comma usage — the strongest measured signal

| Feature | Human | LLM | Unit |
|---|---|---|---|
| Sentences containing at least one comma (essay) | 26.31% | 61.03% | % sentences |
| Average commas per sentence | 1.13% of morphemes | 2.56% of morphemes | rate |
| Relative position of commas | near the beginning | slightly later | relative position |
| Segment length split by commas | shorter | longer | morphemes |

Poetry shows almost no difference in comma usage (sentences are shorter and simpler); paper abstracts show smaller but consistent differences. The single best detection feature, beating spacing and POS n-gram diversity across genres, is **comma-usage patterns** — essays, poetry and abstracts all show it (16.74% / 10.72% / 31.90% improvement over the prior method, respectively).

How Sepia applies it: hunt comma overuse rather than any fixed count. Cut a comma when it does not change the parse or the emphasis (especially the comma after an adverbial-case particle or connective ending, which is the pattern the expert evaluators named in the study). Do not split a sentence to force brevity; the sentence may run on and stay one breath. Do not add a comma to make a passage feel tighter — deletion beats addition, and adding punctuation is the machine habit. Do not floor or cap commas per passage: the genre and the sentence's own weight decide.

### 1b Part-of-speech diversity — LLM structures repeat

Humans use a wider range of grammatical structures and sentence layouts; LLM output repeatedly uses the most probable word combinations. Measured: human essays show higher POS n-gram diversity (1–5 grams) than LLM-generated essays of the same topic; the effect holds across genres.

How Sepia applies it: when one syntax shape repeats (e.g. "[subject]은 [predicate]다" / "[adverb] 따라서 [sentence]" used three or more times in a row), vary the construction — not by inflating it, but by taking the natural short route ("[subject] [predicate]") or the plain participle. Do not force variety by obfuscating.

### 1c Word spacing (띄어쓰기) — LLM is rigid, humans are flexible

Korean spacing is formally rigid (bound nouns must space; auxiliaries attach with a space), but humans systematically deviate: essay and poetry spacing ratios fall below the formal rate, and humans often space or unspace more flexibly, with poetic license. LLM text rigidly enforces the rule — e.g. the bound-noun space ratio in generated essays is highly consistent (SD ≈ 0.02).

How Sepia applies it: do not flag a human-natural spacing choice; flag over-consistency (every bound noun meticulously spaced, every auxiliary meticulously spaced) as a machine-like signal. In an authored draft, mild unspacing in a believable, consistent voice can help; in a review, do not report it as a defect. This is a shape check, not a measured cutoff.

### 1d Repetition and paraphrase — the expert evaluators' top finding

Expert evaluators of the KatFishNet corpus found three things in LLM Korean essays: **(1) excessive comma use, (2) repetition — expressions repeated, or the same sentence restated in paraphrase, (3) linguistic maturity exceeding the expected level** (complex vocabulary, advanced logic, intricate structures beyond the genre and audience). All three are reported as findings, not as style rows:

- Hunt restated content directly. The same point twice, said in different words, is the tell.
- Match the register to the venue and audience. An essay written like a journalism piece (advanced logic, fluent punctuation) overshoots; a casual text written like a textbook is the mirror failure.

## 2 Genre and audience register

Same rule as the rest of Sepia, restated for Korean: state the target register before editing (formal, colloquial, honorific, speech-like, 장편/짧은 글). A Korean thriller and a Korean literary essay should not end up in the same voice. When no register target is given, respect the author's sample text; never impose a register the venue does not want.

## 3 What the English tables do not transfer

The English ban lists in `style-pass.md` §3 do not transfer word for word. What transfers is the *shape* of the checks — run them with Korean words, not the English vocabulary list. Measured Korean-specific shapes:

- **Comma overuse** (1a).
- **Repetition and paraphrase** (1d).
- **Repeated syntax templates** (1b): "X은 Y이다" / "X은 Y다" chains, "그래서 / 따라서 / 이처럼" chains, "[A][B][C]" three-part parallelism.
- **Over-mature language for the genre** (1d).

## 4 Explicitly not measured — Sepia inference

Unmeasured in the Korean literature; treat as hypotheses at the strength of a candidate, not as standing rules:

- Discourse-particle density. Human Korean writing uses particles (-네, -도, -만, -은/는) for natural tone; machine output is often sparer in spoken texture. No measured rate exists. Do not count particles as a verdict.
- Comma placement before vs. after case endings. Measured as "slightly later than humans" for the average; no per-case rule follows.
- Absolute rhythm numbers. Within-vocabulary sentence-length spread is *not* measured for Korean; do not apply a token-length cutoff from the English or Chinese files. Style-pass §5's general check — look for runs of three or more adjacent sentences of about the same length — applies in Korean too, at reading-convention strength, but no numeric or unit-specific cutoff is set.

## 5 False-positive whitelist additions (Korean)

Not evidence of AI:

- A single somewhat long sentence or a deliberate punctuation pause.
- Spacing consistent with the venue's samples, however formally rigid.
- A comma count that happens to be higher than a human median.
- An unusually mature sentence where the venue calls for it.
- Nonstandard spacing the author's own samples use.

What the whitelist does **not** protect: the three measured shapes in §1. A comma cluster, a paraphrase-repeat, or a repeated syntax template is the defect.
